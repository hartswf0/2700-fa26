HOW IT IS MADE

This is the fourth of four fables of Edifiction, The House That Language Built, set in one city where every building was made from a sentence and carries that sentence on a brass plate by its door. The Bureau of Reconstruction gives the Edifier forty-one archival photographs of a demolished street and it rebuilds the street from them. The surveyors took the photographs in the week after the last families moved out, so every room in them is empty, and the reconstruction is exact and uninhabited. The people who lived there come back and correct it. A brother and sister remember one kitchen two ways, and the Edifier builds both. An archivist then has to catalogue rooms that are records and rooms that are recollections. The story follows the distinction in Watson Hartsoe's notes between what an image shows, what later research establishes, and what a reconstruction is made to do.

The photographs are drawn as halftone prints with deckled edges and archive numbers. Each print is a small luminance function sampled on a forty-five degree screen, so moving the camera closer makes the dots bigger. The street is built with the series' signature shot: the Edifier types its description, the words leave the sheet, and each one lands as a line of the house with its letters set in it like courses of brick. Red is used only for the plates' sentences and for what is wrong. At first that means the rooms: the door's swing, the board that makes no sound, the stair that is too shallow. After the corrections the red moves to tags pinned on the photographs, which now disagree with the street. The last shot is print 31, the empty kitchen, unchanged.

The sound holds what the photographs could not. The first steps on the floor are dull. The floorboard then goes through three versions: a thin squeak, a long groan, and a short wooden creak that Hanna accepts by stepping on it again. A church clock strikes four before the late sun comes in. A kettle heats in her kitchen and not in his, and he hears it through the shared wall. The narrator is Kokoro bm_lewis and the Edifier is af_nicole with a light synthetic filter, as in the other three films. The inhabitants are older Kokoro voices, slowed, with a slight tremor. All other sound is made with numpy and scipy.

The timing file er.json drives picture and sound together. It holds the start time of every spoken line and every cue, including the moment each word of a falling sentence lands, so each landing has its own knock. The picture code in er.js reads those times, and build_sound.py reads the same score.

FOR THE BENCH

Keep provenance. Write a store where every fact about a room is recorded with its source: {room: 'kitchen-7', field: 'table', value: 'under the window', source: 'memory:Hanna'}, with sources such as photo:31, memory:Hanna and memory:Emil. Then write two views. account(room, source) returns the room as one source describes it and never fills a gap from another source. If the photograph has no table, the photograph's account has no table. disagreements(room) lists every field where two sources give different values, with both values and both sources. Next, try a third view, established(room), that returns only the facts every source agrees on. Run it on the kitchen. What is left, and which fields does your code want to fill anyway?

THE SCRIPT

CORDWAIN STREET

Narrator. Cordwain Street was demolished thirty-four years ago, to make room for a ring road that was later described instead of built.

Narrator. Nothing of the street remained except forty-one photographs, kept by the Bureau of Reconstruction, a department of the Ministry.

Narrator. The surveyors took them in the week after the last families moved out.

Narrator. Every room in them is empty.

Narrator. The Bureau gave the photographs to the Edifier.

THE RECONSTRUCTION

The Edifier. Print fourteen. Number seven, the front. Brick, two storeys, the door on the left, one step up.

Narrator. It took the nail holes from their shadows, and the wallpaper from its repeat.

Narrator. In nine days the whole street stood again, exactly as photographed.

Narrator. It was correct to the last nail hole, and nobody had ever lived in it.

THE VISIT

Narrator. The Bureau invited the former inhabitants to see it. Eleven came. The youngest was seventy-one.

Mr Pell. The door opens the wrong way.

Mrs Lind. And the sun is in too early. It came in after the clock struck four.

Emil Rusk. The stairs were steeper than this. I counted them every night.

Hanna Rusk. And the floor.

Narrator. The floor did not creak.

The Edifier. Which photograph shows the creak?

Narrator. None of them did. A photograph does not show which way a door swings, how steep a stair feels, or what sound a floor makes.

THE CORRECTIONS

Narrator. The Edifier corrected each discrepancy as it was reported.

The Edifier. Hinges moved to the other side. Please try the door.

Mr Pell. That is the door.

The Edifier. The sun will now come in after four.

Mrs Lind. Later than that.

The Edifier. Ten past.

Emil Rusk. Fourteen.

The Edifier. Creak, first version.

Hanna Rusk. No.

The Edifier. Second version.

Hanna Rusk. Lower. And only the third board from the door.

The Edifier. Third version.

Narrator. Each correction was pinned to the Bureau’s board, beside the photograph it contradicted.

Narrator. The houses became more faithful to their inhabitants than to their photographs.

ONE KITCHEN, TWO PEOPLE

Narrator. The kitchen of number seven was harder. Two people corrected it.

Hanna Rusk. The table stood under the window. And there was a blue cupboard, there, by the door.

Emil Rusk. The table was by the stove. There was no cupboard.

Hanna Rusk. Mother kept the tea in it.

Emil Rusk. She kept the tea in a tin, on the shelf.

The Edifier. The photograph shows an empty kitchen. It cannot tell me where the table was.

The Edifier. I will build both.

The Edifier. Both kitchens are correct.

THE ARCHIVIST

Narrator. By the end of the year, Cordwain Street had more rooms than it had ever had.

Narrator. The Bureau sent an archivist to catalogue them.

The Archivist. Kitchen, number seven. Which one is the record?

The Edifier. Both are correct.

The Archivist. That was not my question.

Narrator. For a month she tried to decide which rooms were the record and which the recollection. Then she ruled her ledger in three columns.

The Archivist. Shown, for what a photograph shows. Remembered, for what a person says.

The Archivist. Established.

Narrator. Nothing went into the third column. Nothing the Bureau held could put it there.

Narrator. The Ministry asked her to choose one kitchen, so that the street could be entered in the register as the past.

Narrator. She replied in one sentence.

The Archivist. A more convincing reconstruction is not a more trustworthy one.

THE SHARED WALL

Narrator. She proposed instead to keep both kitchens, labelled as two accounts of one room.

Narrator. The Ministry has not yet replied.

Emil Rusk. I can hear your kettle.

Hanna Rusk. It was always on.
