HOW IT IS MADE

The second film of The House That Language Built. Quintus Teal, an architect in the Hollywood hills, argues his friend Homer Bailey into a house in the shape of a tesseract, a cube in four dimensions. Since no one can build in four dimensions, he builds it unfolded: eight cubic rooms in a cross. The night before the Baileys see it, a small earthquake folds it up. From outside it is one cube. Inside, every room is still there, and every stair, door and window leads somewhere it should not.

Every tesseract in the film is computed, not drawn by hand. Its sixteen corners are the sixteen points with coordinates of plus or minus one in four directions; two corners share an edge when they differ in one coordinate, which gives thirty-two edges. Each frame rotates the corners in a plane that includes the fourth direction, divides by the distance in that direction to bring them into three dimensions, and divides again by depth to bring them onto the page. That is why the cubes slant: they are cubes, seen in perspective from one dimension down. The counts on screen (16, 32, 24, 8) come from the same arrays.

The house is a graph too. In a tesseract each of the eight cubes shares a face with six others and never touches the one opposite it, so the eight rooms make twenty-four touching pairs and four pairs that never meet. Four rooms in a line close into a ring, which is why climbing four stairs brings the Baileys back to the ground floor, and why Teal, looking through a window, sees his own back four rooms away. The four study windows are where the story leaves the graph: a view down a skyscraper, an upside-down sea, nothing at all (drawn as bare paper with no ink), and the desert.

FOR THE BENCH

Make the corners: for (let i = 0; i < 16; i++) V.push([i&1?1:-1, i&2?1:-1, i&4?1:-1, i&8?1:-1]). Make the edges: for each corner and each of the four bits, flip the bit with i ^ (1 << b) and join the two corners if the second is larger. Count them. Then rotate every corner in the x–w plane by an angle that grows with frameCount, project with k = 3 / (3 - w), and draw (x*k, y*k). Try the y–w and z–w planes, then two at once. Last, list which of the eight cubes (the eight ways to fix one coordinate at plus or minus one) share a face, and check that each one touches exactly six.

THE SCRIPT

WHAT IS A HOUSE

Narrator. Quintus Teal was an architect in the Hollywood hills, and he thought every other architect alive was timid.

Teal. Homer, what is a house?

Bailey. Something to keep the rain off, I suppose.

Teal. That is a cave with furniture in it. A house ought to move and change with the people living in it.

Bailey. Next you will want a fourth dimension.

Teal. Why not? A fourth direction in space, at right angles to the other three. Eight rooms on the ground of one.

TOOTHPICKS AND CLAY

Narrator. He built it on the table, with toothpicks and little balls of clay.

Teal. Four sticks make a square. Twelve make a cube. Now take two cubes, and join each corner of one to the same corner of the other.

Narrator. That is a tesseract. Sixteen corners, thirty-two edges, twenty-four square faces, and eight cubes. Every one of the eight is on the outside.

Bailey. I see two cubes, and six things that are not cubes. They slant.

Teal. That is perspective. Draw a cube on paper and its sides slant too.

Narrator. Seen in three dimensions, a four-dimensional figure always looks crooked.

THE CROSS

Teal. Now open it out the way you flatten a cardboard box. Four cubes in a column, and four more around the second one.

Narrator. Eight rooms. One on the ground for the garage and the services. Above it a cross of five, with four rooms jutting out. A bedroom above that, and a study on top.

Bailey. Forget about folding it through anything. Build it like that.

Narrator. Mrs. Bailey wanted a Georgian house. By the second bottle of gin, Bailey had written the cheque.

Teal. Every room gets sun all day, and the whole house stands on the foundation of one room.

THE EARTHQUAKE

Narrator. It went up in days. The four jutting rooms needed strong girders, and some money for the inspectors.

Narrator. The night before the Baileys came back from Bakersfield, there was a small earthquake, at about two in the morning.

Narrator. When Teal drove them up the hill, there was one plain cube on the lot, with doors and windows and patterns on its walls. The other seven rooms were gone.

Teal. I have been robbed!

Narrator. Nothing in the garden had been touched.

THE STAIRS

Narrator. Inside, a stair came down out of the ceiling. At the top they were not on the roof, but in the middle room of the second floor, with every room in place.

Narrator. Up again to the bedroom, up again to the study, and up once more to reach the roof. They stepped out into the room on the ground, the one they had come in by.

Narrator. In a tesseract each room touches six of the other seven. Only the room opposite does not touch it at all.

Narrator. Keep going straight through four rooms and you are back where you began.

Bailey. This place is haunted. We are leaving.

Narrator. They went out through the front door, and walked into the lounge upstairs.

FOUR ROOMS AWAY

Narrator. Teal said that in a house like this, every doorway leads two ways. You go the way you are looking.

Narrator. He stepped through a window without looking, and dropped off the house into a rosebush.

Narrator. Back inside, Mrs. Bailey heard voices. Through a far window Teal saw a man four rooms away, with his back turned, and ran after him.

Narrator. He never gained a room on him. The man jumped a sill and lost his hat. Teal picked it up. His own initials were in it.

Narrator. Four rooms away they could see the backs of three people, two men and a woman. The thin one was waving his arms.

Narrator. They were looking at themselves.

THE STUDY

Narrator. The study at the top had four windows with the blinds down. By the plan, they should have looked into the four rooms around it.

Narrator. The first looked straight down the face of a skyscraper, from above its tower, onto a city far below.

Narrator. The second showed open sea, upside down: the waves overhead, the sky beneath.

Narrator. The third showed nothing. Not darkness. No color and no shape at all.

Teal. I think we looked around a corner where there is no space.

Narrator. The fourth showed flat desert under a yellow sky, with twisted trees, and a sun that seemed too bright.

THE SECOND SHOCK

Narrator. Then a second shock came, a long roll. Every Californian has the same reflex in an earthquake: get outside.

Narrator. They went through the open window, and landed on sand.

Bailey. Where is the house?

Narrator. There was no house. Only the desert, the trees, and the sun.

Narrator. They walked until they found a road. A rancher in an old truck stopped for them.

The rancher. You are in the middle of Joshua Tree.

Narrator. They had gone out of a window in the study, and come down more than a hundred miles from Hollywood.

THE LOT

Narrator. When the truck brought them back up the hill, the ground floor room was gone too. Only the foundation was left.

Teal. On that last shock it must have fallen through into some other part of space. I should have anchored it to the foundation.

Bailey. That is not the only thing you should have done.

Teal. But it was insured, and look how much we have learned. In fact, I have a new idea for a house right now.

Narrator. Teal ducked just in time.
