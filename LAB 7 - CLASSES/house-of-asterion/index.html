The House of Asterion

HOW IT IS MADE

The third film of The House That Language Built. Asterion speaks about his house: its doors are never shut, it has no furniture, every part of it repeats, and it is as big as the world. He describes his games, among them showing an imaginary second Asterion around the house. He describes the nine men who come in every nine years, and the redeemer one of them promised. The last scene belongs to someone else: Theseus, in the morning, with Ariadne.

The film draws the house the way Asterion describes it: fourteen by fourteen courtyards, each with the same pool, opening the same way on all four sides, on a torus, so that walking off the right edge brings you in at the left. Every place looks like every other place. The film keeps a set of all the courtyards Asterion could be standing in, given the steps he has taken and what he has seen at each one. He knows his own moves, but with nothing to tell courtyards apart, every step moves the whole set along with him and the count stays at 196, which is why the other Asterion can be led wrong. After the nine men have fallen and stay where they fell, there are nine marked courtyards. On the same walk the count goes 187, 178, 9, 9, then 1. The bodies are what let him know where he is.

Asterion is the jointed puppet of the earlier films with a bull’s head. The four faces in the redeemer scene are the four the story asks about. Theseus’s sword shows no blood, as the story says; the figure lying far back in the gallery is drawn small and in shadow.

FOR THE BENCH

Make a 14 by 14 grid where the right edge joins the left and the bottom joins the top: a step from x goes to (x + dx + 14) % 14. Keep an array B of every cell. Each time the walker moves, move every cell in B by the same step; then keep only the cells where what the walker sees now (a body or no body) matches what is there. Print B.length. With no bodies on the grid it never changes. Add one body and walk across it, then nine, then try to place the nine so that the count falls to 1 as fast as possible.

After Jorge Luis Borges, “La casa de Asterión” (1947), in Labyrinths: Selected Stories and Other Writings, edited by Donald A. Yates and James E. Irby. Paraphrased; nothing is quoted. Download the film (mp4).

THE SCRIPT

THE DOORS

Asterion. I know what they say of me. That I am proud, that I hate people, that I am mad. I will deal with that in time.

Asterion. Yes, I stay in my house. But its doors are never shut, at any hour, to any man or any animal. There are more doors than anyone can count, and not one lock.

Asterion. There is no furniture here, and no court. There is quiet, and room to be alone.

Asterion. They say I am a prisoner. There are no locks.

THE STREET

Asterion. Once, one afternoon, I did go out into the street. I came back before night.

Asterion. Their faces frightened me. Flat and colorless, like the palm of a hand.

Asterion. A child cried. People prayed, ran, lay face down. Some climbed the temple steps. Others picked up stones.

Asterion. They knew who I was.

THE LETTERS

Asterion. My mother was a queen. I cannot be taken for one of the crowd.

Asterion. I never learned to read. I could never keep one letter apart from another.

Asterion. Sometimes I am sorry, because the days and the nights are long.

THE GAMES

Asterion. I have my games. I charge down the stone galleries with my head down, like a ram, until I fall over dizzy.

Asterion. I crouch in the dark beside a pool, or round a corner, and pretend someone is after me.

Asterion. There are roofs I let myself drop from, until I bleed.

Asterion. I pretend to sleep, eyes shut, breathing hard. Sometimes I do sleep, and when I open my eyes the light has changed.

THE OTHER ASTERION

Asterion. The game I like best is the other Asterion. I pretend he has come to visit, and I show him round.

Asterion. I bow, and tell him: now back to the first crossing. Now out into another courtyard. The drain, I knew it would please you.

Asterion. But every courtyard is like every other. When I say the first crossing, it could be any of them.

Asterion. Sometimes I lead him wrong, and we both laugh.

THE HOUSE IS THE WORLD

Asterion. I have thought about the house. Each part of it comes again and again. Every place is some other place too.

Asterion. There is not one pool, one courtyard, one trough, one manger. There are fourteen of each, which is to say there is no end of them.

Asterion. The house is as big as the world. No: it is the world.

Asterion. Once, at the far end of the galleries, I came out on the street, the temple, the sea. Then a vision in the night showed me that seas and temples repeat as well.

Asterion. Only two things seem to happen once. Above, the sun. Below, Asterion.

THE NINE

Asterion. Every nine years, nine men come into the house, so that I can free them from all evil.

Asterion. I hear their steps far off in the galleries, and I run to them, glad. It is over in a few minutes.

Asterion. They fall, one after the other. I do not have to bloody my hands.

Asterion. They lie where they fell. With them there, one gallery is no longer the same as the next.

THE REDEEMER

Asterion. I never learn their names. But one of them, as he died, said that one day my redeemer would come.

Asterion. Since then the loneliness does not hurt. I know he is alive, and that one day he will stand up out of the dust.

Asterion. If I could hear every sound in the world, his footsteps would reach me. I hope he takes me somewhere with fewer galleries, and fewer doors.

Asterion. What will he be like? A bull, or a man? A bull with a man's face? Or like me?

MORNING

Narrator. In the morning the sun flashed on the bronze sword. Not a trace of blood was left on it.

Theseus. Can you believe it, Ariadne? The Minotaur hardly fought back.
