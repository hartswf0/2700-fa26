HOW IT IS MADE

The third film of The House That Language Built. Asterion speaks about his house: its doors are never shut, it has no furniture, every part of it repeats, and it is as big as the world. He describes his games, among them showing an imaginary second Asterion around the house. He describes the nine men who come in every nine years, and the redeemer one of them promised. The last scene belongs to someone else: Theseus, in the morning, with Ariadne.

The film draws the house the way Asterion describes it: fourteen by fourteen courtyards, each with the same pool, opening the same way on all four sides, on a torus, so that walking off the right edge brings you in at the left. Every place looks like every other place. The film keeps a set of all the courtyards Asterion could be standing in, given the steps he has taken and what he has seen at each one. He knows his own moves, but with nothing to tell courtyards apart, every step moves the whole set along with him and the count stays at 196, which is why the other Asterion can be led wrong. After the nine men have fallen and stay where they fell, there are nine marked courtyards. On the same walk the count goes 187, 178, 9, 9, then 1. The bodies are what let him know where he is.

Asterion is the jointed puppet of the earlier films with a bull’s head. The four faces in the redeemer scene are the four the story asks about. Theseus’s sword shows no blood, as the story says; the figure lying far back in the gallery is drawn small and in shadow.

FOR THE BENCH

Make a 14 by 14 grid where the right edge joins the left and the bottom joins the top: a step from x goes to (x + dx + 14) % 14. Keep an array B of every cell. Each time the walker moves, move every cell in B by the same step; then keep only the cells where what the walker sees now (a body or no body) matches what is there. Print B.length. With no bodies on the grid it never changes. Add one body and walk across it, then nine, then try to place the nine so that the count falls to 1 as fast as possible.

After Jorge Luis Borges, “La casa de Asterión” (1947), in Labyrinths: Selected Stories and Other Writings, edited by Donald A. Yates and James E. Irby. Paraphrased; nothing is quoted. Download the film (mp4).
