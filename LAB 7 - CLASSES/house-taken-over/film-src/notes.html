HOW IT IS MADE

This is the first film of The House That Language Built, three films after short stories in which a house is made, unmade or ruled by the way it is described. A brother and his sister Irene, both near forty, live alone in a big old family house. One night he hears something behind the oak door at the end of the corridor, shuts it and bolts it, and the two of them live on the near side. Later the sound comes from their own side, and they leave through the iron gate and the front door with nothing.

The film reads the house the way a program would. The plan is a graph: each room is a node, each door an edge. What the two of them have is every room that can be reached from Irene’s room without going through a shut door. The film computes that with a breadth-first search, and the red wave on the plan is the search spreading one door at a time. With the oak door open, fifteen rooms. Shut, eight. After the iron gate, one, the vestibule. After the front door is locked and the key is gone, none. Whoever takes the house over is never drawn and never counted as anything but a shut door; they are only heard, through walls, as a knocked chair and a low conversation.

The people are the jointed puppets of the earlier films. The plan stays in the top corner of every scene in the house, so the count is always on screen. The sound keeps the house’s small noises: a clock, two knitting needles, a kettle, a bolt, a drawer, plates, stamp-album pages, breathing at night, and the sounds from the other side, filtered as if through a door.

FOR THE BENCH

Write the house as data: an array of rooms and an array of doors, each door naming the two rooms it joins and whether it is shut. Then write reach(start): put the start room in a queue, and while the queue is not empty take a room out, look at every open door it touches, and add the room on the other side if you have not seen it. Return how many rooms you saw. Now shut one door and run it again. Find the one door whose shutting loses the most rooms; that is the oak door. Then give the house a second way round to the back (a door from the kitchen into the dining room) and see how the answer changes.

After Julio Cortázar, “Casa tomada” (1946), translated by Paul Blackburn as “House Taken Over”. Paraphrased; nothing is quoted. Download the film (mp4).
